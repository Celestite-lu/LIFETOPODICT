# a b s t r a c t

Dictionary learning has been widely recognized as an effective method for processing high-dimensional and nonlinear data. Although graph structures have been widely adopted to enhance optimization in irregular data handling, existing approaches inadequately utilize local graph information, leading to suboptimal dictionary update. Consequently, the learned dictionary struggles to capture local details effectively, which can negatively impact the performance of classification tasks. To tackle this problem, this study proposes a method based on graph structure, dubbed Deep Neighbor Atom-enhanced Dictionary Learning (DNADL), designed to enhance the sensitivity of dictionaries to local structure. Our DNADL segments data into internally consistent neighborhood manifold module via clustering method and enhances dictionary atoms based on their self-expression relationships, where the self-expression relationships within generated manifolds reflect the intrinsic correlation of local data structures. Enhanced dictionary atoms exploit the similarities among neighboring data points, ensuring that the constructed dictionary effectively captures local structure features. Furthermore, this study introduces an adaptive graph learning module that dynamically updates graph representations, thereby facilitating the simultaneous optimization of dictionary learning and graph topology within an integrated framework. Comprehensive experimental results demonstrate that DNADL achieves state-of-the-art classification performance across multiple benchmark datasets, with accuracies of 99.23 % on Fashion-MNIST, 94.12 % on EMNIST, and 95.3 % on Oxford Flowers 102. By effectively capturing local structural features, DNADL surpasses existing methods by a significant margin of 0.98 % to 3.70 % in accuracy.

# 1. Introduction

The advent of big data presents new challenges to traditional signal processing methods. Over numerous scenarios, the signals being processed often exhibit nonlinear and high-dimensional characteristics, which hinder the effectiveness of conventional methods designed for low-dimensional spaces. Dictionary learning (DL) has been considered as a powerful representation learning method for processing highdimensional and nonlinear data in signal processing and data representation, which has been extensively utilized across a range of visual tasks, such as image classification (Qu et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024), scene recognition (Liu et al., 2018b), face recognition (Mokhayeri & Granger, 2020), anomaly recognition (Liu et al., 2024) and so on. The objective of DL is to develop a dictionary capable of efficiently representing data by expressing signals as sparse linear combinations of its base components. This method effectively captures key features from highdimensional data, reducing redundancy and improving the processing of complex visual information. The K-SVD approach (Aharon et al., 2006) is one of the most classic and widely used algorithms in DL, aiming to minimize reconstruction errors by iteratively updating both the dictionary and the sparse representation. This method employs a unique dictionary update mechanism to compress data and extract the most recognizable key features. Subsequently, the improved D-KSVD (Zhang & Li, 2010) and LC-KSVD (Jiang et al., 2011) algorithms were proposed, which use class labels to enhance dictionary quality or reduce learning complexity. However, these methods mentioned above ignore the dependencies generated by irregular data domains, which means that the learned dictionary itself may not have an effective structure or explicitly integrate the underlying topological structure. As certain signal characteristics, such as continuity, depend on the topology of the graph, the topological structure of the signal should be considered in order to identify and leverage the inherent structure. This is especially significant in scenarios where data is incomplete, insufficient, or corrupted. Therefore, there is a critical need to incorporate prior knowledge provided by the underlying graph structure into DL.

To address this need, numerous scholars have proposed methods to incorporate the geometric structure of graph signals into DL. One strategy is adding graph-related regularization terms (Rico et al., 2024) to the optimization objective. For example, Zeng et al. (2023) augmented the dictionary model by integrating a graph generation function that relies on radial basis function kernels and Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) distance. Adding this integration to the regularization term promotes the generation of nonlinear embedding spaces.

Yankelevsky and Elad (2016) proposed a dictionary learning approach tailored for multi-label scenarios, introducing a new graph Laplacian regularization that explicitly incorporates the graph structure of the data improves the discriminative ability of the learned sparse representation. Another strategy focuses on deep level graph signal decomposition. Gou et al. (2024) proposed a novel hierarchical locality-aware deep DL (HILADLE), which can learn locality constraint dictionaries at different abstract graph levels through hierarchical DL. Yang et al. (2023) proposed a new discriminative semi-supervised graph learning via deep and dictionary representation (DSSLDDR), which involves leveraging both the discriminative nature of dictionary representations for graph signal reconstruction.

Nonetheless, the two aforementioned strategy can only be considered as incorporating the graph structure to a limited extent in DL. Whether by adding graph regularization (Yang et al., 2021) terms to the optimization objective or by decomposing and reconstructing graph signals, these methods offer only marginal support for dictionary updating section. In other words, they fall short of fully utilizing the local information inherent in the graph. If we pursue a DL method centered on graph structure which embedding it into core processes such as sparse encoding and dictionary update, graph information would evolve from being a supplementary component to becoming the central driving force of the optimization process. By dynamically adjusting the weights or graph structure, DL could achieve deeper integration with the characteristics of the graph, significantly enhancing its effectiveness.

In this paper, we develop a neighborhood self-expressive manifold learning module as the graph-structured starting point for DL. We employ the clustering algorithms, such as ??-means, to identify cohesive clusters within the data. Derived from these clusters, the dataset is partitioned into distinct neighborhood manifolds. Although data distributions in high-dimensional spaces are typically nonlinear, our model approximates the data manifold as a linear subspace (Baek et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024) within sufficiently small neighborhoods. Under this framework, the data exhibits strong local consistency.

Every data point can be expressed as a linear combination of other points within its neighborhood. The self-expressive nature of this relationship ensures the model’s robustness against interference from distant, irrelevant data points. This enables the model to concentrate on local image features within limited sample ranges while ensuring smooth data updates.

To achieve this, this study proposes a novel graph Laplacian-based dictionary atom enhancement mechanism, called Deep Neighbor Atomenhanced Dictionary Learning (DNADL). During dictionary updates, this mechanism uses a Laplacian matrix constructed from neighboring manifolds to implement self-expression relationships. Specifically, it assigns self-expression weights to dictionary atoms through Laplacian matrix operations. This weighted enhancement helps the updated dictionary atoms better capture local data distributions, ultimately improving the quality of reconstructed images.

To tackle the possible discrepancy between the graph structure and the dictionary resulting from multiple dictionary iterations, this study proposed an adaptive graph learning module. This module ensures continuous learning of the enhanced dictionary atoms by dynamically adjusting the graph structure based on the progress of dictionary iterations. It uses latent representations obtained through sparse coding in DL as input for layer-wise graph optimization. By simultaneously performing adaptive learning (Liu et al., 2018a) on the graph structure, the module supports the next steps in DL. This approach achieves joint optimization of DL and the graph structure, enhancing the dynamic adaptability of the manifold module.

In summary, the primary contribution of this work is threefold:

• To address the challenge of nonlinearity in high-dimensional data representation, this study proposes a neighborhood self-expression manifold module. This graph-structured framework partitions data into cohesive clusters via clustering algorithms and prepare for subsequent dictionary learning work by extracting potential selfexpression relationships.   
• To enhance local feature preservation during DL, this study develops a graph Laplacian-based dictionary atom enhancement mechanism. By integrating a Laplacian matrix derived from neighborhood manifolds, this method assigns self-expression weights to dictionary atoms, ensuring alignment with local data distributions.   
• To ensure dynamic adaptability between graph structure and dictionary atoms, this study designs an adaptive graph learning module. This component jointly optimizes graph structure and dictionary update through layer-wise sparse representation guidance, achieving dynamic adaptability of graph structure.

The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 offers a summary of related work. Section 3 describes the proposed approach in detail. Section 4 demonstrates the model’s effectiveness through experiments on various datasets. Lastly, Section 5 provides the conclusion of the paper.

# 2. Related works

# 2.1. Graph regularized dictionary learning

In previous research, DL has demonstrated efficacy in uncovering graph structures and encoding sparse correlations, particularly in domains such as image segmentation, graph data analysis, and facial recognition. Over recent years, dictionary learning has developed deeper methods and applications in different directions. Zhou et al. (2024) proposed a new data-driven tight frame (DDTF) dictionary learning approach with an over-complete dictionary formed by discrete cosine transform (DCT). Liu and Ma (2019) proposed a novel structured graph dictionary learning (SGDL). This approach ensures that the dictionary atoms, derived from groups of similar patches, remain consistent and smooth, suggesting that any signal expressed using this dictionary will also exhibit smoothness. Some existing discriminative DL methods have also introduced graph structure or manifold techniques to enhance the performance and generalization ability of the model (Chen et al., 2024). Du et al. (2020) proposed a new low-rank graph preserving discriminative DL approach (LRGPDDL) for sparse representation-based image recognition. A discriminative graph-preserving criterion and a discriminative reconstruction error term are utilized to capture discriminative information, significantly boosting the discriminative power of the learned dictionary.

In addition, the combination of DL and graph Laplacian regularization can better integrate local and global information and obtain stronger the self-expression ability of dictionaries. Yan et al. (2018) introduced a dictionary learning method with cross-view graph Laplacian regularization, which preserves geospatial structural consistency between original data and latent subspace through adaptive graph constraints. Zhou et al. (2023a) developed a multidictionary induced LRR method (MDLRR) to obtain a better low-rank structure by forming a penalty dictionary imposed on the data set. Gao et al. (2023) proposed a manifold-based low-rank dictionary pair learning (MLRDPL) approach for set-based video recognition or imagesets classification tasks. It is evident that most methods related to ensemble manifold learning and dictionary learning rely on dictionary constraints, typically maintained as static throughout the learning process. However, the static nature of such constraints will present limitations, as data distribution and characteristics vary over time or across tasks, necessitating models with adaptable capabilities. In contrast, by shifting our focus towards leveraging self-expression (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2017) relationships within neighborhood manifolds to update dictionary atoms, we can impart the model with enhanced dynamic adaptability, facilitating superior accommodation to evolving data conditions.

# 2.2. Deep dictionary learning

Deep dictionary learning (DDL) (Gou et al., 2023; Tariyal et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2023b) has been extensively applied to a variety of pattern recognition tasks, owing to its robust feature extraction capabilities, and has achieved remarkable success. Generally, Shallow dictionary learning focuses on a single dictionary and coefficients, while DDL expands with multiple layers using deep learning techniques. DDL employs a greedy layer-by-layer strategy for better data representation. Its end-toend training method allows direct learning from raw data, applicable in various domains like signal and image processing. This strategy can help avoid getting stuck in local optima and allow for effective parameter optimization at each layer. Tang et al. (2021) presented a novel DDL and Coding Network (DDLCN) tailored for image recognition tasks under data constraints. The foundational convolutional layers within DDLCN were substituted with innovative compound DL and coding layers. Goel and Majumdar (2022) proposed a transformation rooted in the principles of DDL, which integrates the Sparse Subspace Clustering (SSC) loss into the DDL framework. During the network’s training phase, the information of the neighborhood relationship graph can be combined with the loss function of the deep dictionary model to guide the network to learn more representative and generalized feature representations (Liu et al., 2013). Compared with shallow dictionary learning, DDL serves as a remedy for the limitations inherent in shallow dictionary learning models, which exhibit static characteristics and thus fail to adapt to feature variations. Gao et al. (2022) proposed a novel multimodal convolutional DL approach that efficiently correlates various image modalities and thoroughly takes into account neighborhood information at the image level. Zheng et al. (2021) introduced a new network model called deep convolutional dictionary learning (DCDicL), which learns the priors for both representation coefficients and dictionaries, and has the capability to adaptively adjust the dictionary for each input image according to its content. While these method have made efforts to integrate graph structure techniques into deep dictionary learning, aiming to improve the model’s capability in processing intricate and abstract data representations through multi-level learning, there remains a pressing

need for heightened data sensitivity, particularly concerning the intricate details of local data structures.

In atom-enhanced dictionary learning stage, self-expression relationships are formalized via Laplacian regularization constraints within these manifolds (Theodorakopoulos et al., 2016) (Li et al., 2017), encoding local structural patterns into a weight matrix ?? that quantifies both similarity and contributory dependencies between data points. Subsequently, model will gradually generalize the entire reconstruction error process by introducing ?? , forming an enhanced network for dictionary learning. Simultaneously, sparse coding results from this stage are employed as the input of the adaptive graph learning module, which computes graph deviation term Δ?? between the original Laplacian and recovered features. Based on the graph deviation term and the original Laplacian graph, we perform adaptive graph updates to replace the original Laplacian graph and assist dictionary learning in completing the learning process. This closed-loop cooptimization mechanism ensures that both dictionary atoms and graph structure evolve synergistically, enabling robust capture of local geometric details while maintaining global consistency. Finally, the sparse representation ?? generated by the deep enhanced dictionary network is utilized as the input to train label-specific classifiers for classification tasks (Jiang et al., 2025).

# 3.2. Atom-enhanced dictionary learning

In the process of constructing neighborhood manifolds using the spectral clustering algorithm, we have derived the Laplacian matrix ??. As previously mentioned, the Laplacian matrix not only plays a crucial role in determining the number of neighborhood manifolds, but it also directly contributes to the enhancement of dictionary atoms. This is because the Laplacian matrix is a core product of the graph structure. It is well known that within a partitioned neighborhood manifold, there exist sufficient self-expression relationships that aid in strengthening dictionary learning’s ability to capture local details. Self-expression relationships describe the contribution of surrounding data points to a central data point within a given range, i.e., they measure how much a data point can be expressed in terms of its neighbors. This effectively restores the objective distribution of data under a local model. When the Laplacian matrix is employed as a means of standardizing dictionary atoms, it ensures that the similarity between dictionary elements adheres to the local structure of the data, thereby making the dictionary atom representations smoother. Here we propose a method for utilizing graph structure information to assist in updating dictionary atoms, specifically by transforming the Laplacian matrix to derive a weight matrix that represents neighborhood relationships:

$$
\Sigma_ {i, j} \| x _ {i} - x _ {j} \| _ {2} ^ {2} W _ {i j} \Leftrightarrow 2 \mathrm{Tr} (X ^ {T} L X). \tag {5}
$$

In the above formula, the left expression can be interpreted as a distance measure that relies on the graph structure, where $W _ { i j }$ denotes the weight matrix, and $| | x _ { i } - x _ { j } | | _ { 2 } ^ { 2 }$ represents the Euclidean metric between two sparse representation atoms. In the expression on the right, ?? is the Laplacian matrix, and ?? corresponds to the sparse representation obtained through the Laplacian operator. In the context of DL, the above formula must satisfy the condition that the trace operation between the Laplacian matrix and the sparse representation matrix can be expressed as the sum of squared weighted distances between elements within the sparse representation matrix. More precisely, by quantifying the relationships between data points within the graph structure and assigning distinct weights to dictionary atoms based on the weighted distance differences between data points, the objective of minimizing the reconstruction error is achieved.

In $\mathrm { D L } ,$ , researchers aim for the linear combination of the dictionary ?? and the sparse representation ?? to approximate the original sample data ?? , thus minimizing the reconstruction error. If we incorporate the weight matrix $W _ { i j }$ into the dictionary ?? during the error reconstruction process, we essentially transform the graph information advantage into a form of reinforcement for the dictionary. In the dictionary update iteration stage, combining the weight matrix $W _ { i j }$ with dictionary atoms becomes a critical aspect of performing self-expression weighting on dictionary atoms, thus facilitating original dictionary atom translating into enhanced dictionary atoms. The weighted dictionary atoms can be denoted as $D _ { i } ^ { R }$ :

$$
D _ {i} ^ {R} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {l} W _ {i j} D _ {j}, \tag {6}
$$

where $D _ { i }$ denotes ??-th column of the dictionary ??, which can be defined as one dictionary atom, $W _ { j }$ denotes ??-th row of the weight matrix $W _ { i j }$ and ?? indicates the number of dictionary atoms. The above equation indicates that when updating a dictionary atom $D _ { i } ^ { R } ,$ , weight information from neighboring or similar samples is introduced. However, it would be unreasonable to directly replace the original dictionary atom with this updated version, as we ideally want the dictionary atoms to moderately focus on the weighted components. Otherwise, the dictionary would become overly focused on local features, thus neglecting the importance of global optimization. Additionally, since the weighted dictionary atom is derived from a transformation of the original dictionary atom, $D _ { i } ^ { R }$ shares similar feature semantics with $D _ { i } .$ . We represent the enhanced dictionary atom as $\hat { D } _ { i } ^ { \phantom { \dagger } }$ , which is expressed as a linear combination of the initial dictionary atom and its weighted neighbor dictionary atom. The parameter ?? represents the coefficient that governs the degree of influence from the neighboring sample information:

# 3.4. Adaptive graph learning module

In dictionary learning with graph structure, graph Laplacian is used to capture the similarity and local structure between data points. The update of dictionary atoms typically relies on the existing graph Laplacian, which influences the local adaptability of the dictionary. However, as the dictionary and sparse coefficients are updated, the data distribution and local manifolds may undergo subtle changes, meaning that the originally constructed graph may no longer fully adapt to the current dictionary. Therefore, this study develops an adaptive graph learning module to allow the graph structure to continuously adjust with the update of the dictionary and data, ensuring that the dictionary more accurately reflects the local manifold structure of the data.

The concept of the adaptive graph learning module leverages the idea of stacked learning blocks, accumulating multiple learning stages to help the dictionary learning process obtain new sparse representations. After obtaining an enhanced dictionary, we must reconsider the similarity between data points and adjust the graph structure accordingly, thereby obtaining a new sparse representation. In our work, we build a three-layer deep dictionary learning network, where the dictionary is reduced in dimensionality after each layer iteration, allowing us to ultimately obtain the most representative feature representations. During the network propagation, a graph deviation term is formed at each layer based on the reconstruction error, which is aiming to help updating the graph.

Building upon the robust enhanced dictionary obtained in prior steps, we now focus on dynamically adjusting the updated graph to align with a new sparse representation. Specifically, the solution of the new sparse representation $X _ { i }$ serves as the optimization target for adapting the graph update, thereby validating the effectiveness of sparse coding in conjunction with graph update. The objective function is formulated as follows, comprising two key components: the first term computes the reconstruction error after transforming the sparse representation $X _ { i - 1 }$ from the previous layer using the graph Laplacian, while the second term acts as a regularization term for the graph Laplacian structure, ensuring the adequacy and reasonableness of local structure considerations. If the elements of $X _ { i }$ exhibit strong local structural coherence, this term will be small. Conversely, significant structural variations will result in a larger term, thereby imposing a stronger penalty:

$$

# 4.1.3. Evaluation for classification tasks

In classification tasks, Accuracy, Precision, and Recall are widely used metrics to evaluate model performance. Accuracy quantifies the ratio of correctly classified samples to the total number of samples and is expressed as

$$
\text { Accuracy } = \frac {\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{TN} + \mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{FN}}, \tag {21}
$$

where TP, TN, FP and FN indicate True Positives, True Negatives, False Positives, and False Negatives, respectively. While accuracy provides an overall performance measure, it may be misleading in imbalanced datasets where one class dominates. Precision quantifies the proportion of correctly predicted positive samples among all samples predicted as positive and is calculated as

$$
\text { Precision } = \frac {\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}. \tag {22}
$$

Precision is particularly important when the cost of false positives is high. Recall quantifies the ratio of correctly predicted positive samples to the total number of actual positive samples and is expressed as

$$
\text { Recall } = \frac {\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}. \tag {23}
$$

Recall is crucial when the cost of false negatives is high, such as in medical diagnosis or fraud detection. These metrics provide complementary insights into model performance, and the choice of metric depends on the specific application and the relative importance of false positives versus false negatives.

# 4.5. Layer-wise analysis of DNADL

To examine the effect of network depth on the performance of DNADL, we performed experiments to evaluate classification accuracy across different numbers of dictionary layers. In particular, the model was assessed using layer configurations spanning 1 to 5 layers with the MNIST and Fashion-MNIST datasets. For fairness, the total number of dictionary atoms across all configurations was fixed at 700 (e.g., 700 atoms for 1st-layer, 350-350 for 2nd-layer, 233-233-234 for 3rd-layer, etc.). The training settings remained consistent with Section 4.1, and the classification accuracy on the test set was adopted as the evaluation metric. The training parameters were maintained in accordance with Section 4.1, using classification accuracy on the test set as the evaluation criterion.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the accuracy rises with the addition of layers, peaking at 3 layers (99.65 % on MNIST and 99.23 % on Fashion-MNIST), but declines when further deepening the network. This phenomenon can be attributed to two factors: (1) Shallow networks (1-2 layers) lack sufficient hierarchical representation capacity to capture both global and local structural features; (2) Excessively deep networks (4-5 layers) introduce redundant parameters, leading to overfitting on limited training samples. The 3-layer configuration strikes an optimal balance, enabling multi-granularity feature extraction while maintaining model generalization. These results validate our design choice of a three-layer architecture in DNADL.

# 5. Conclusion

In this work, we develop the Deep Neighbor Atom-enhanced Dictionary Learning method (DNADL) to tackle the challenge of dictionary learning’s limited capacity to capture local data structures in classification tasks. Our approach involves constructing neighborhood manifolds on data samples and updating dictionary atoms with rich self-expression relationships. We replace the original dictionary with updated atoms that combine self-expression information and incorporate neighborhood manifold constraints to fully explore the model’s ability to capture local data structures. Furthermore, we extend our method to the realm of deep dictionary learning by introducing adaptive graph learning module to enhance the performance of our model. Through the joint optimization of deep dictionary networks and adaptive graph learning module, the resulting dictionary exhibits strong feature expressiveness. Comprehensive classification experiments performed on benchmark datasets reveal that our proposed method outperforms state-of-the-art approaches.
